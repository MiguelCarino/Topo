# Topo

A network register you draw. Put down the devices, their addresses and the cables between them; Topo checks the network as you go (address conflicts, switching loops, gateways and DNS servers a device cannot reach) and turns it into a printable site report.

**[Open it →](https://topo.carino.systems/)** — runs in your browser, nothing to install, no account.

![Topo editor showing a small home network with its subnet and the device palette](docs/screenshot.webp)

## Two views

- **Simple** (`#simple`, or `#imagenology` for a medical imaging site) is for someone who is not a network technician: plain words, a short device palette, and only names and connections to fill in. A problem the checks find shows as a mark on the device. It is in English, Spanish, Portuguese (Brazil), Japanese and Russian.
- **Full editor** (`#advanced`) has every field, the Subnets, Groups, Wi-Fi and Findings tabs, the Table and Rack views, bulk editing and the spreadsheet round-trip. Its technical vocabulary (Gateway, DNS, VLAN, Interfaces…) stays in English on purpose; its sentences, the findings and the site report are translated.
- **Setup** (`#setup`) asks how many of each kind of device you have and draws a network around a router.

Switching views never loses anything; they show the same document.

## The register

- **Devices.** About twenty types (router, switch, firewall, access point, server, VM, PC, printer, IP camera, phone, imaging modality and more). Each device has interfaces with an address and a MAC, a gateway, DNS servers, open ports and an asset group: model / firmware, serial number, asset tag, location, status (planned, active, retired) and notes. In the full editor a new device starts with nothing it was not given: no address, gateway, DNS server, model, allowed ports or NAT. Its address comes from the subnet it is cabled into, and its gateway and DNS from that subnet.
- **Retired devices** stay in the register, dimmed on the map. Nothing reaches them or passes through them, their own checks are skipped, and their address is free for a replacement without raising a conflict.
- **Subnets** (full editor, Subnets tab) are objects with an address, a name, VLAN, purpose, gateway, DNS servers and DHCP on or off, and show their size, use, next free address and members. A device belongs to a subnet by its address and inherits the subnet's gateway and DNS unless it sets its own; routers and firewalls keep their own. **Renumber** moves a subnet to another range in one step and refuses when the new range overlaps another subnet, is too small, or would put two devices on one address. Subnets in use that nobody declared are listed with **Declare**. Each subnet's zone can be drawn on the map: always, never or automatically for private ranges; a private subnet nobody declared is drawn automatically too. A zone is drawn round its own devices (a router on several subnets does not stretch them), and two automatic zones whose devices interleave are not drawn. In the full editor "subnet" is always one of these, and "network" the whole document.
- **Groups** (full editor, Groups tab) say where devices sit: a building, the floors in it, the rooms on them and the racks in those, or a group of your own. Each device is in at most one group, and it is also in every group above that one. The tab lists the tree with how many devices are in each group, and has Select members, Edit (name, kind, the group it sits inside, and a rack's size), + Inside and Delete. A rack's size is its height in units (4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 27, 42, 45, 47 or 48, or Custom…), its width (10″, 19″ or 23″, which the rack view draws it in proportion to, except that a 10″ rack is kept wide enough for a device's name to read), its depth in mm if you give one (it is printed, not checked) and its numbering: from the bottom, as most rails are, or from the top. The numbering only changes the numbers shown (on the rails, in a device's `U12`, its path, the findings and the report) and moves no device; the device editor's U and the place dialog take the number printed on the rails, which on a rack numbered from the top is the device's top unit. The spreadsheet's `rack_u` stays counted from the bottom either way, so a sheet means the same unit however the rack is numbered. A new height keeps every device on the units printed beside it: the units come and go at the top of a rack numbered from the bottom and at the bottom of one numbered from the top. Made shorter than a placed device, it asks first, naming the devices in the units it would lose; they then wait in the rack under Not placed yet. That holds wherever the height is changed: the rack view, the Groups tab, a table cell, a pasted column (its preview counts the devices that would lose their place, and Apply asks) and an imported groups sheet (its review counts them). Deleting a group leaves its devices in no group, or deletes them too if you choose; the groups inside it move up a level. The device editor's Asset section has a Group picker, and for a rack the device's lowest unit, its height in units, the face it is on (front or rear) and a Full depth box, ticked by default: every device in a rack fills the rack front to back unless you untick it for a device on one face only (half depth), such as a patch panel or a PDU at the back. It also has **Ports face**, the side of the device its ports are on, which the rack view draws them on. A rack drawn before this default opens with all its devices full depth, so a device on the front and one on the rear that shared units are now an overlap; the finding says so and that both have to be set to half depth to share them. Where a device's location is shown (the table, the spreadsheet, the report, printed labels), a device in a group shows its group's path, such as `Building A / Server room / Rack 3 · U12–U13 front`. A device in no group shows its typed location as before. A network whose devices have locations typed in but no groups yet is offered **Make groups from locations**: it splits each location on ` / `, `>` or `,` into a tree, shows that tree first, and makes it in one undo step. The typed locations are kept as they were.
- **Groups on the map.** Each group with devices draws a dotted frame round them with its kind, name and device count on a tab. A group's frame sits inside the frame of the group it is in. A frame is not a subnet zone: zones are tinted and labelled with an address. The ▾ on a frame's tab collapses the group into one node that shows its kind, name and count, and the worst finding among its devices with how many there are. Cables with both ends inside a collapsed group are not drawn, and cables to the outside are drawn once per far end, marked ×N when there are several. Collapsing changes only the drawing: the checks, the table and the report still cover every device, and each device keeps its place, so expanding puts them back exactly. A click or tap on a collapsed node (or its ▸) expands it, and an expanded frame keeps the ▾ on its tab to fold it again. Dragging a collapsed node, or a frame's tab, moves the group's devices as one undo step; a press counts as a drag only once the pointer has moved a few pixels (more for a finger), so a tap never nudges the group. To select the group's devices, Shift-click the collapsed node, click an expanded frame's tab, or use Select members in the Groups tab (the way on a phone). **Drop in, drop out:** drag a device (or a selection) and let go inside a frame, and it goes into the innermost group under the pointer; let go outside every frame and it is in no group; let go on a collapsed node and it goes into that group, which stays collapsed. While you drag, the frames hold still, the target is lit and a label at the pointer says where the devices will go; a drag that ends inside the device's own frame only moves it. A group whose devices are all in the dragged selection travels with it and keeps them (so dragging the whole map with Ctrl+A changes no group). The move and the change of group are one undo step. Out of a rack a device leaves its units behind; into another rack it keeps them where they are free there, and otherwise waits under Not placed yet, which the message says; into a rack from anywhere else it has no units until you place it. **A new group from two devices:** drag a device (or a selection) and let go on top of another device's card, one in no group or in the group the dragged devices are all in, and Topo offers to make a new group of them: the device under it is ringed with a dashed green line and the label says "New group with …" while it is over it (a frame's target is lit in the accent colour, a host's in blue). The question asks the name and the kind, guessed from where it goes (a group of your own at the top, a floor in a building, a room on a floor, a rack in a room when every device is rack gear); the suggested name follows the kind until you type your own. Make group puts both in the new group, inside the group they share when they share one, with the dragged devices set beside the other rather than on it; Cancel only moves them, left where they were let go. Either way the move and the rest are one undo step. A device in another group is not offered (its frame takes the drop), nor one that runs on a host, nor two devices in a rack, which sit in units rather than in groups. Let go beside a card rather than on it, a device only moves. A finger drags a device on the map as the mouse does, with the same drops, once it has moved past a tap; a tap still selects. The Group picker and Move to group do the same without dragging. Which groups are collapsed is kept in this browser per network. It is not part of the network, so it does not go into files or links. The search, the trace, Zoom to matches and a click on a table row reach devices inside a collapsed group through its node. The PNG, SVG and report diagram show groups collapsed or expanded as they are on screen. The simple views draw no frames.
- **Rack view** (full editor, Map | Table | Rack, shown while the network has a rack). A rack picker, a Size button with the rack's size fields (the same as the Groups tab's), and the rack's units numbered as its rails are, front and rear side by side (a Front/Rear switch on a phone). Each placed device is a box over its units with its icon, name and status. Each device shows its ports in a strip along its box, on the face they are on, evenly spaced in order (not where they are on the real faceplate): a switch's or access point's ports named as the box names them, 24 to a row, and another device's interfaces, a bond's members side by side under a bar. A free port is hollow, a cabled one is filled (in the cable's colour when it has one), one a finding is about (two cables on a port that takes one, past the faceplate's count, or a cable a finding names) is ringed, and one with an access VLAN is tinted. Several cables on a router's or firewall's interface are not ringed: like the findings, the rack view reads that as a gateway with a switch not drawn. A 1U box at 1× has no room under its name, so its ports are a hairline along its foot instead, one dash per port (faint when free, the cable's colour when cabled, red when a finding is about it), clear of the name. Which side of the device its ports are on is its **Ports face**: network gear (switches, routers, firewalls, access points, load balancers and the like) has them on its front and everything else on its back unless you say otherwise, in the device editor's rack section or with the Ports button beside the selected device's name; a half-depth device shows them on its own face, so for one of those the button and the field are greyed and say so. Point at a port, or tap one on the selected device, to read its name, label, VLAN and bond and its cable: the far device and port, the cable's tag, colour, length and via. The selected device's box grows over its neighbours so its ports can be read and tapped: its own units stay tinted inside it, and it steps in from the outer rail so the devices it is drawn over keep their edge and icon in sight there, where a tap selects them; and the 1× / 2× / 3× switch makes every unit taller (kept in this browser, never in the network). Drag over empty units, or tap one, to add a device there. The dialog says where it goes in one line (Change opens the units, face and depth). **New device** is a grid of the types that go in a rack, server first, with an optional name and **Add**; **From the map** lists the devices already drawn, this rack's unplaced ones first, and opens first when there are some. Drag a device to move it, drag the top edge of the selected one to change its height, or use the arrow keys and the buttons under it. Each change is one undo step. Units that are taken are refused. A device takes its units on both faces (full depth, the default; its back is drawn on the other face) unless it is set to half depth with the Full depth button under it, the device editor's box or the place dialog's. The dialog's box is for a new device and is unticked by itself where only one face is free (worked out again as you edit the dialog's unit, height or face); a device already on the map keeps its own depth there (a half-depth one is marked so in the list), going in at half depth only where one face is all that is free, until you tick or untick the box yourself, which then sets the depth of whatever you pick. Where a device on one face and another's back are in the same units (an overlap), the back is drawn as a thin strip on the outer rail, so a click there always selects the device mounted on that face. Each cable is drawn from its port to the port at its other end: down to the box's lower edge, along it to the cable manager at the side of that face (the column between the faces; the rear is drawn as the front's mirror, with its numbers on its outer rail), up or down the manager on a track of its own so lines do not stack, and along the other device's lower edge into its port, in the cable's colour when it has one. A cable from a port on the front to one on the rear goes round through the manager. A cable to a device that is not placed in this rack runs to the manager too and on its own track up or down to the rack's top or bottom edge (the nearer), where it ends in a ring, so the cables from one device to several elsewhere are each a line of their own. A cable whose end has no port picked (or names one the device does not have) meets the box at its **no-port spot**, a dashed ring at the end of its ports; tap it to see those cables, each with **Pick a port**, which opens the port picker in the rail (one undo step), and the selected device's buttons have Pick a port too. A VM's or a container's cable meets its chip in its host's box. The **Cables** switch picks what stands out: **Selected** (the default) draws the selected devices' cables bold and tagged and the rest faint; **All** draws every cable, with their tags from 2× up; **One cable** traces one cable end to end: tap a port, a line or a tag. The choice is kept in this browser, never in the network (a patch schedule row switches to One cable for that visit only). A tag is a small flag hung under each port the cable leaves, with the cable's label, else its ports (`eth0 ↔ 24`), else where it goes (`→ PC-12 · eth0`); tags never cover one another nor another box's name, ports or chips, and a tag on show stays where it is while you point at it (one with no room near its port is left off; the port's tip still says it all). Point at a line or a device to light its cables and tags; tap a tag or a line to select the cable and show the port's tip: the tag, both ends, VLAN, length, colour and via. On a phone, which shows one face, a cable to the other face runs in the manager on its own track to the far device's level and ends in a ring there, tagged with the device, port and face there; the selected device's buttons sit in one row that scrolls sideways, so the rack keeps most of the screen, and a device tapped is kept in sight when they appear. On a phone or a narrow window the view scrolls as one column with the units stuck to its top (about a third of the screen or more): scroll down to Not placed yet or Other devices and their rows come up just under the units, ready to drag in; a device tapped then brings its buttons back into sight. Drag from a free port to a free port on another device to make a cable bound to those two ports, in one undo step (a finger drags from the selected device's ports). Let go on another device's box instead of a port, or drag from a device's dot to another device, or use Cable to…, and the far port is picked the way the map picks it, with the port picker open to change it. Selection is shared with the map and the table. To bring a device in, drag it from **Not placed yet** or from **Other devices** (every device not in this rack, with a filter) onto the units: it takes its own height there, and a device from elsewhere joins the rack. A finger drags a row by its handle; Place on a row does the same with a tap, at the lowest free units. A full-depth device brought in where only one face is free (behind a half-depth device) goes in at half depth, and a message says so. Near the top or the bottom of the units' pane, a drag scrolls it, so a device can be moved to units out of sight. To take one out, drag a placed device off the units onto one of the two targets that appear beside it (under the units, on a narrower window): **Not placed yet** keeps it in the rack without units, **Out of the rack** moves it into the group the rack is in (or no group). Let go anywhere else and it goes back. The selected device's Out of the rack button does the same with a tap. **Patch schedule:** beside the rack (under it on a narrower window) is the rack's list of cables, a row per cable that touches it: the device here and its port, the cable's tag (or "no tag", so the untagged ones the findings count are found at a glance), the far device and its port (and where it is, when that is not this rack), the colour, the length and the via. A cable between two devices of the rack is read from the higher one, and the rows run down the rack as it is read, a device's ports in order. Sort by any column with its head (again for the other way; blanks stay last); the sort is a view of this visit. Click or tap a row to select its cable (the rail shows it) and trace it in One cable mode, with the rack scrolled to bring its ends into sight; a cable selected on the rack lights its row. On a phone the schedule is the main view: the rack stays stuck above it as you scroll, and each row is stacked to fit, with the column heads as sort buttons. Patch panels, power, weight and device models are not drawn.
- **VMs and containers on their hosts** (full editor). A VM or a container says what it runs on with **Runs on** in its Asset section: a VM runs on a server (a hypervisor or a NAS is drawn as one, so servers are offered first), a workstation or a custom device, and a container on one of those or on a VM, one level deep; nothing runs on a container, and nothing on network gear, phones, printers, cameras or a cloud. A device with something on it lists what runs there, a VM's containers under the VM, and a name selects that device. A VM or a container is where its host is: it is in its host's group (its own Group picker shows the host's, greyed; let go into a rack with no host, the toast says to set its Runs on, not Open rack), moving the host into another room or rack takes it along, and in a rack it takes no units of its own (it is at the host's units, so it never overlaps one). Its place on the map and its cables stay its own. A host that is deleted, or a guest turned into another kind of device, leaves it running on nothing, where it was. **On the map**, drag a VM or a container (or a set of them) onto a device it may run on and let go: it runs there and is set in the nearest free place beside it (never on its card), in one undo step (the device is ringed and a label says "Runs on …" while it is over it, in a colour of its own, so it is never mistaken for a drop into a group or a new group, which a VM let go on a device it cannot run on is offered instead); a guest dragged anywhere else only moves. A finger does the same on a phone, and Runs on does it without dragging. A host carries a badge with how many run on it; a click or tap on the badge folds them into the host, drawn as a second card behind it, with their cables drawn to it once per far end (×N), and again brings them back. A folded host follows a collapsed group's rules: a click or tap on its card unfolds it (and selects nothing), a drag moves it, still folded, and Shift-click selects it; unfolded, a click selects it as any device. A fold lasts only while something runs on the host: when its last guest goes (the hosting undone, Runs on set to none, the guest deleted or turned into another kind of device), the fold goes too, so a VM dropped on it later is drawn beside it, and a redo brings the guests back unfolded. Like a collapsed group, folding is a view kept in this browser: the search, the trace, Zoom to matches and a table row reach a folded guest through its host, and Delete asks first. **In the rack view** a host draws what runs on it as chips in its box, a VM's containers after the VM, "+N" for those it has no room for (the place dialog offers no VM or container among its new device types for the same reason); a click on a chip selects that device (its bar has Select its host and Runs on nothing). Drag a VM or a container from Not placed yet or Other devices onto a placed device to run it there (it has no Place: units are not its to take, and let go on empty units, or on a device it cannot run on, it is refused, with a hint); drag a chip onto the Runs on nothing target beside the rack to take it off its host, where it waits under Not placed yet, or onto another device to move it there (held at the top or foot of the pane, the drag scrolls it to a host out of sight); a finger drags a chip too. A host's box with more than it has room for shows as many chips as fit, at least one, and "+N". A cable to a guest is drawn to its host's box, labelled with the guest's name.
- **Wi-Fi.** A network keeps its Wi-Fi networks as a list of their own, like the subnets: each with its SSID, security (Open, OWE, WPA2, WPA3, WPA2/WPA3, the Enterprise modes, and WEP and WPA kept only so an old site can be written down), its bands (all of them unless it says otherwise), the subnet it puts clients on (and so its VLAN) and whether it is hidden. There is no passphrase field. A wireless interface's radio has a standard, shown by name with the IEEE letters (Wi-Fi 6 (802.11ax), Wi-Fi 6E, Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be)), and the bands it works on; on an access point each band has a channel, a width and a power, and broadcasts every SSID on that band unless it lists its own. A new access point is Wi-Fi 6 on 2.4 and 5 GHz with channels on auto; any other radio starts blank, which means unknown. A wireless link records its SSID, band and signal in dBm; unless someone picks them, the SSID is the access point's only one, or, when it broadcasts several, the one whose subnet holds the client's address, and the band the best one both ends (and the SSID) have.
- **Wi-Fi in the editor** (full editor). The **Wi-Fi tab** sits next to Subnets and Groups whenever the network has an access point, a wireless link or a Wi-Fi network. It shows one card per network with its security, bands, subnet and VLAN, the access points that broadcast it and how many clients it has, plus **Select clients**, Edit and Delete. With no networks yet it offers **Staff**, **Guest** and **IoT**, one click each, and the ones not made yet stay under the cards as one-click chips. Staff goes on the subnet named for it, or on the only subnet there is; Guest and IoT go only on a subnet named for them. A **wireless link's panel** shows SSID, Band and Signal (dBm) where a cable's label, length, colour and via would be. Each select starts with what Topo works out ("Auto · Staff"), and Band lists only the bands both ends have. Under them is the access point's channel and width on that band, with **Edit radio**. A typed signal of 67 is read as −67, since a phone's number pad has no minus key. A VPN link has no cable facts either; any stored ones are kept but hidden. Under a wireless interface, the **radio** folds to one line ("Wi-Fi 6 · 2.4 GHz ch 6 · 5 GHz ch 36/80 · Staff, Guest"). Opened on an access point, it has the standard, a row per band with a channel and a width picked from the ones valid for the band (also for the bands that come from its standard, before any is ticked), and the SSIDs it broadcasts as chips. A client's radio has only the standard and bands; bands that come from its standard show greyed until changed.
- **Cables** carry a label, length, colour and what they run through (`PP-A:12 > WJ-3B`), since patch panels and wall jacks are not drawn as devices.
- **Ports** (full editor). A switch or access point can name its ports the way the box does (`Gi1/0/` from 1 makes port 12 `Gi1/0/12`). A prefix cannot end in a digit. Each port can carry a label (the jack or desk at the other end) and an access VLAN. A port's VLAN is recorded, not modelled: trunks, tagged VLANs and PoE are not, and the checks do not split the network by VLAN.

## Checks

- Duplicate IP and MAC addresses, gateways outside the device's subnet or out of its reach, DNS servers it cannot reach, cables whose two ends disagree (IPv4 and IPv6), switching loops that need STP, a cable label or asset tag used twice, and notes that look like a password. For groups: groups that are each other's parents, a device past the top or below the bottom of its rack, two devices on the same units of the same face (or a full-depth device and anything behind it), and rack units on a device that is not in a rack. And, for information only, how many cables in each rack have no tag: listed in the Findings tab, but not counted on the pill, the tab, a card or the report, since nothing is wrong on the network. For VMs and containers: one whose host is retired, and one in another group than its host (which the editor never leaves; a file can).
- For Wi-Fi: a client on a band its access point's radio does not offer (or its own radio does not have), 6 GHz without Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 at both ends, a radio channel its band does not have or a width the band or the standard cannot take (these three on the radio itself mark the device), a client set to an SSID its access point does not broadcast (or not on that band), an SSID whose subnet does not hold the client's address, two access points on overlapping channels of one band in the same room or floor (with neither in any group, on the same subnet; two in groups but no shared room or floor, such as different buildings, are not flagged; the channel's width counts), an open, WEP or WPA (TKIP) network that an access point broadcasts (OWE is fine), and a signal weaker than −75 dBm, or −67 dBm for a VoIP phone. For information only, a 2.4 GHz channel other than 1, 6 or 11. What nobody filled in (a standard, a channel, an address, a signal) is unknown and never raises anything. Channels are compared between access points (and home routers) only, never a client's recorded channel, and a width the band cannot take counts as 20 MHz there. A wireless link between two access points (a mesh backhaul) has its band checked but is on no SSID, so its address is never tested against one. Security findings are one per network and overlap findings one per band, and accepting one is kept to that network or band: accepting Staff's WEP does not accept Guest turning open on the same access points, and accepting an overlap on 2.4 GHz does not accept one on 5 GHz. Otherwise one check on the same devices is one finding, so accepting it cannot quietly accept another.
- One set of checks feeds the marks on the map and, in the full editor, the device's Diagnostics tab, the **Findings** tab and the report, so they agree. The Findings tab groups findings by check and folds the same finding on many devices into one line; clicking a line selects the device and centres the map on it. A pill on the map counts what is open; A opens the tab and goes back.
- **Accept…** keeps a finding you know about, with a required reason. It stops counting everywhere (tab, pill, badge, saved card, report totals, bulk previews) and is listed under Accepted with its reason; Reopen takes it back. What is accepted is those devices: a new device joining the same conflict is open again.
- **Trace** (full editor: Diagnostics tab, or T) shows everything the selected device can reach, optionally through one port.

## Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi is recorded where it lives: the networks as a list of their own (the Wi-Fi tab), the radio on each wireless interface, and the SSID, band and signal on each wireless link (see The register above). Most of it fills itself in: a new access point is Wi-Fi 6 on 2.4 and 5 GHz, a link takes the access point's SSID and the best band both ends have, and only what differs needs typing.

- **On the map** (full editor) a wireless link carries its band ("5 GHz") as a small chip on its own line, toward the client end and placed clear of subnet names, devices, other chips and a selected link's ✕, so it is always plain which link it belongs to; its tooltip is the SSID and the signal, and a tap on it (or just round it) selects the link. A 6 GHz link is drawn in a finer dash of the same purple with a filled chip, so a Wi-Fi 6E or 7 association stands out. The purple is a token (`--topo-wifi` in css/app.css). Tracing still sends the ))) arcs down every radio link.
- **In the report**, a Wi-Fi section after Connections: a table of the networks (SSID, security, the bands it is broadcast on by its access points, else the bands it is set to, subnet and VLAN, the access points broadcasting each and its clients), then a block per access point with its standard and a row per band of each of its radios (an access point with one radio per band shows each, named by its interface): channel and width ("auto" when left to the access point), the SSIDs on it and each client on it with its signal in dBm. A network with nothing wireless has no Wi-Fi section.
- **The starting points** have real Wi-Fi with no findings: the Smart Home's Wi-Fi 6 access point broadcasts Home and an IoT network on 2.4 GHz for the camera and the printer; Retail has a guest network on its own subnet, the Hotel one guest network on both floors' access points, and the Showcase's office a Wi-Fi 6E access point whose 6E laptop lands on 6 GHz by itself. Common Errors adds one Wi-Fi fault to find: an open guest network.

## Working with many devices

- **Selection.** Shift- or Ctrl-click adds or removes a device or cable, Shift-drag boxes devices in, Ctrl+A takes everything, and a subnet's or a group's **Select members** takes everyone in it. A selection moves, deletes and undoes as one step.
- **Change all of them** (full editor, two or more devices): set a field on all of them (a field they differ on says "mixed"; blank leaves it alone; Clear empties it so the subnet's value applies again); rename them with a pattern such as `CAM-{n:02}` or find and replace; move them to a subnet (each takes the next free address); move them to a group (a device leaving a rack leaves its units behind; a VM on a host stays with its host, and a host's VMs go with it); put their VMs and containers on a host, or on none (a VM on a VM is refused on its row); set the SSID and band of the ones on Wi-Fi (**Wi-Fi**, offered only when the set has a device on Wi-Fi; Auto takes them back to what Topo works out, and a band the two ends do not share, or an SSID the device's access point does not broadcast, is refused on its row); re-cable them onto another switch or access point (each moves to the next free port, keeping its cable's label and its own address); or delete them. Every change shows first what would change and what the checks would find afterwards (for example "+2 Duplicate IP, −24 Gateway"), then applies as one undo step. Selected cables get the same treatment for their own fields.
- **Map | Table** (full editor): the same network as rows on six tabs (Devices, Interfaces, Cables, Ports, Subnets, Groups), and a seventh, Wi-Fi, whenever the rail offers its Wi-Fi tab. With Wi-Fi, Interfaces also shows each radio's standard, bands and channels, and Cables each wireless link's SSID, band and signal (greyed while they are worked out rather than set). A wireless or VPN link shows a dash for the cable's facts. The Devices tab has a Group column that takes a group's path (greyed on a VM on a host, which is where its host is) and a Runs on column that takes the host's name or id on a VM or a container; the Groups tab has a rack's height, width, depth and numbering. Edit a cell in place; a value that is not valid for its column is refused. Sort by any column, move with the arrows and Tab, and click a row to find it on the map. **Paste a column** lays a spreadsheet column down the ticked rows, or from the cursor's row downward, with a preview first. On a phone the table opens first.
- **Search** (full editor) works on the map and the table: plain words match any field (and an SSID finds the devices broadcasting it or on it), and `type:`, `ip:` (an address or a network such as `10.10.30.0/24`), `mac:`, `vlan:`, `switch:`, `status:`, `loc:`, `group:` (a group's name or part of its path, matching the devices in it and in the groups inside it), `host:` (a host's name or id, matching what runs on it and on its VMs), `tag:`, `ssid:` (an access point that broadcasts it and the devices on it), `band:` (`2.4`, `5` or `6`), `wifi:` (a standard: `wifi:6e`, `wifi:ax`, `wifi:7`), `channel:` and `has:` (`finding`, `group`, `host` for what runs on a device, `guests` for a device something runs on, `wifi` for a radio or a wireless link, and more) narrow it; `-` leaves a word out. A word it does not understand is reported under the box. **Zoom to matches** and **Select matches** act on the result.
- **Add devices**: count each kind, give a name pattern (`Phone-{n:02}`, numbered after the names already on the map) and choose the switch or access point. The new devices take its next free ports and the next free addresses of its subnet, inherit the subnet's gateway and DNS, and arrive selected as one undo step. The dialog says first which names, ports and addresses they will get, warns when they would not fit on the switch, and warns when a pattern without `{type}` would number every kind in one run. Devices joining an access point by radio join its Wi-Fi network. When it broadcasts more than one, the dialog asks which, starting on the first that is not a guest network, and the devices take their addresses from that network's subnet. A switch on a router port with no address gets no addresses from the router's other ports. Started from the landing, it builds a new network around a router whose LAN every switch is cabled to.
- **Blocks** (full editor) are saved selections: shipped ones (a DMZ, a VoIP closet, a Wi-Fi cell…) or your own. Inserting one moves each of its subnets to the next free one of the same size, so a second copy never conflicts with the first.
- **Tidy** lays the map out in tiers (internet, router, switches), then each switch's devices in port order, at most 12 to a row, with each switch above its own devices and each tier above the one it serves. With groups, each group's devices are laid out as a block inside its parent's, and no two blocks overlap. With two or more devices selected it arranges only those.
- Undo and redo work per action: a typed field, an inserted block, a batch of added devices or a Tidy is one step each.
- **Motion.** A collapse, an expand, a Tidy, an undo or redo and a drop are played rather than jumped to: devices glide to their new places, fly into a group as it collapses and spread out of it as it opens, a frame eases to its new size and the one a drop went into pulses once. In the rack view a box that is moved or resized slides to its units and the cables fade back in. Only the picture moves; files, links and exports are the same either way. It is off when the system asks for reduced motion.

## Spreadsheets and pasted output

- **Export** (full editor, File → Spreadsheet): CSV sheets (devices, interfaces, cables, ports, subnets, Wi-Fi once the network has a Wi-Fi network, groups, findings) in UTF-8, or the same rows copied as TSV. The subnets sheet carries each subnet's name, VLAN, gateway and DNS, which devices inherit, so subnets, devices, interfaces (with bonds) and cables imported into an empty map give the whole network back. The devices sheet has `group` (the group's path), `rack_u`, `rack_height`, `rack_face`, `rack_depth` (`full` or `half` for a device in a rack), `ports_face` (`front` or `rear`, the device's own or its type's; one that says its type's is not stored) and `runs_on` (a VM's or a container's host by its name, or by its id when another device has the name; on import a host further down the same sheet is found too, and a host it may not run on is said on the row and left out) columns; a depth column headed "Half depth" or "Shallow" reads a yes as half depth, any other depth column a yes as full; on import, groups the sheet names that the map does not have are made only after you tick the box for them. The groups sheet holds each group's id, name, kind, parent and a rack's height, width, depth in mm and numbering (`bottom` or `top`); a sheet that makes a rack shorter says in the review how many placed devices lose their units. The cables sheet carries everything the rack view's patch schedule shows (both ends' devices and ports, and the cable's label, colour, length and via): a switch or access point port left blank on a new cable's row is read as no port there (the rack view's no-port spot), not one picked for it, so a cable nobody patched yet comes back unpatched, while a blank port on a device's own NIC is still its NIC; and the devices sheet's `switch` and `port` columns leave out a cable with no port at the switch, which the cables sheet carries instead. The Wi-Fi sheet holds each network's SSID, security, bands, subnet (by its CIDR) and hidden flag; the interfaces sheet carries each radio's standard, bands and, per band, channel, width, power and the SSIDs it broadcasts (`ch_2g`, `width_5g`, `ssids_6g`…; an SSID is written by name, or by its `wifi_id` when the name would read back as something else: shared by two networks, holding a `;`, or reading `none`); the cables sheet carries each wireless link's `ssid`, `band` and `signal`. Import the subnets and Wi-Fi sheets before the sheets that name their networks. A cell that would start a formula (`=`, `+`, `-`, `@`) goes out with an apostrophe in front and comes back without it. A sheet exported and imported back changes nothing.
- **Import** takes a CSV file or rows pasted from a spreadsheet. Columns are matched by heading (`Hostname`, `IP Address`, `MAC Address`, `Switch`, `Port`…) or, without headings, by what the values look like, and you can change any match. Rows are matched to devices by id, then asset tag, then MAC, then IP, then name (a name two devices share is a conflict, not a guess). A row found only by its address or name that names another device (another asset tag or serial, or two of another MAC, name and type) is a conflict, not an edit of the device that holds that address. Only mapped columns are applied and a blank cell changes nothing. A row that matches nothing adds a device, cabled to the switch and port it names. A sheet of ports sets labels and VLANs on ports already drawn and never cables anything. A sheet of subnets (a `cidr` or `network_id` column) declares or edits subnets; it never moves one to another range, which is Renumber's job.
- **Paste anything**: the same box reads an ARP table or a DHCP lease list (Linux, macOS, Windows, Cisco, HP/Aruba, Juniper, MikroTik, dnsmasq, ISC dhcpd, Fortinet…) and takes each address with its MAC and, when given, a name. A name seen on the network never renames a device you named, and is never used to match one, because DHCP names repeat.
- Every import goes through a review that lists each row as added, changed, unchanged or a conflict, with what the checks would find afterwards. Apply is one undo step.

## Reports, labels and exports

- **Site report**: a printable document with a header you fill in (site, client, author, reference, date, scope; a blank site is the name the network is kept under), the findings with accepted ones listed separately with their reasons, an inventory with one line per interface (a host's VMs and containers listed under it, stepped in), the inventory under a heading per group when there are groups, an elevation of each rack (front and rear, numbered as its rails are, what runs on each host beside it and listed under the rack, as wide as the rack against a 19″ one, under its size, each device with a small strip of its ports on the face they are on, free hollow and cabled filled, then the rack's patch schedule, a row per cable in the rack's order with both ends, the tag or "no tag", the colour, the length and the via), a port map for every switch and access point (with labels and VLANs when the ports have them, and free ports as ranges), a Wi-Fi section when the network has anything wireless (see Wi-Fi below), what changed since the last back-up, and the diagram (on its own page past 40 devices).
- **Print labels**: 62 × 29 mm labels for a selection. Each tagged device gets its tag, name, address and MAC with a QR code of its asset link, drawn in the page; each labelled cable gets two, one per end.
- **Asset links**: `topo.carino.systems/#asset/<tag>` opens the network in this browser that holds that asset tag and selects the device.
- **Images**: PNG, WEBP, and SVG (full editor) with the site's name as its title and nothing in it that reaches outside the file.
- **Netplan**: on a PC, server, VM or container, **Open in Netplan ↗** opens [Netplan](https://netplan.carino.systems/) filled in with that device's interfaces.
- Downloads are named after the site and the date (`clinica-norte-2026-10-26.nettopo.json`).

## Keeping your work

- A network you start here (New network, Add devices, a file you open, a saved card) is kept in this browser and saves itself as you work; the bar on the map says when it last saved. The example and a link someone sent you are not stored until you press **Keep this network**.
- **Back up** saves one network as a `.nettopo` file, or all of them in one binder file. After a back-up or a report, each card says how much has changed since ("± 3 changes since the last back-up": devices, cables, subnets, Wi-Fi networks, groups, notes, accepted findings and the report header) and lists the changes on request. That comparison copy stays in this browser and never goes into a file, link or binder.
- If the browser refuses to save (storage full or blocked), a banner stays up with **Back up now** until a save gets through.
- Opening a file of a network you already have offers to update it (the newer one wins) or to open it as a copy.
- The starting points show groups: the Data Centre is a building with a data hall holding two 42U racks (every device placed, a half-depth switch on the back, the web tier as VMs and a container on the app servers) and an ops room; the Imaging Centre is filed by room with a 24U rack in its server room; the Call Centre has a 12U wall rack of half-depth gear; the Smart Home is filed by room. Under Examples, the Showcase files its tower by floor and room, with two racks in the server room, a wall rack in the call centre, the API container inside the web VM on the app server, and the branch office and the home as groups of their own.
- Saved templates are cards in the same library, with Duplicate to start a network from one.
- Every saved network records the version of the format it was written in. Older ones are upgraded when opened; one from a newer Topo opens read-only, so saving it here cannot drop what this version does not know. A read-only network can still be inspected, reported on and exported as it arrived.
- Two tabs open on the library see each other's saves.

## Privacy

Topo has no server and uploads nothing. Kept networks live in this browser's storage (IndexedDB, falling back to local storage where IndexedDB is not offered), and the network you are working on is held in the page's history, not in the URL. A share link carries the whole network and is locked with a passphrase by default (AES-GCM); before a network leaves as a link or a report, Topo names any device whose notes look like they hold a password. A Wi-Fi network has no passphrase field, so a Wi-Fi key cannot travel in a file, a link or a sheet. The only outside request comes from the optional status panel, which looks up your public IP address when you open it.
